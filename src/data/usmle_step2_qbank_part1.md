### Question 1

A 68-year-old man with a history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes presents to the emergency department with sudden-onset tearing chest pain radiating to the back. His blood pressure is 185/100 mmHg in the right arm and 150/80 mmHg in the left arm. ECG shows no ST-segment changes. Chest X-ray reveals a widened mediastinum.

**What is the most appropriate next step in management?**

A) Administer IV tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)  
B) Obtain CT angiography of the chest  
C) Perform emergent echocardiography and prepare for pericardiocentesis  
D) Start heparin infusion and admit to the cardiac care unit  
E) Obtain serial troponins and repeat ECG in 6 hours  

---

**Domain:** Cardiovascular System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions (immediate management in emergency)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate-Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — CT angiography of the chest**

*Why it's correct:* The presentation is classic for aortic dissection: tearing/ripping pain radiating to the back, blood pressure differential between arms, widened mediastinum on CXR, and risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, older male). CT angiography of the chest (CT angio) is the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis, defining the extent of dissection (Type A vs Type B), and guiding surgical vs medical management. It is fast, widely available, and highly sensitive/specific.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "TEARING = Type A Emergency, Radiate Into New Geography"** — Tearing pain + BP differential + widened mediastinum → think aortic dissection → CT angio first, NOT tPA.

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (tPA):** tPA is for ischemic stroke or massive PE — giving it in aortic dissection would be catastrophic, worsening hemorrhage into the pericardium or mediastinum.
- **C (Pericardiocentesis):** Tamponade can complicate Type A dissection, but pericardiocentesis before confirming the diagnosis can worsen hemopericardium. CT angio comes first unless the patient is in extremis.
- **D (Heparin):** Anticoagulation is contraindicated in aortic dissection — it increases bleeding risk without benefit.
- **E (Serial troponins):** This approach is appropriate for suspected ACS, but the clinical picture here (tearing pain, BP differential, widened mediastinum) points strongly away from ACS and toward dissection.

### Question 2

A 34-year-old woman at 32 weeks gestation presents with severe headache, visual changes, and right upper quadrant pain. Her blood pressure is 162/110 mmHg. Labs show: platelets 88,000/µL, AST 210 U/L, ALT 195 U/L, LDH 680 U/L. Peripheral blood smear shows schistocytes.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A) Administer magnesium sulfate and plan delivery  
B) Administer nifedipine and schedule delivery at 37 weeks  
C) Initiate corticosteroids and expectant management until 34 weeks  
D) Perform emergent cesarean section without further delay  
E) Admit for observation and repeat labs in 24 hours  

---

**Domain:** Pregnancy, Childbirth, & the Puerperium  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions  
**Difficulty:** Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: A — Administer magnesium sulfate and plan delivery**

*Why it's correct:* This patient has HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) complicating severe preeclampsia, evidenced by thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases, elevated LDH, schistocytes, and severe-range blood pressures with symptoms. The definitive treatment is delivery. Magnesium sulfate is given for seizure prophylaxis (eclampsia prevention). At 32 weeks, corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity should also be administered, but delivery should not be delayed beyond stabilization — certainly not to 34 weeks when the mother is this ill.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "HELLP = Hemolysis Elevated Liver Low Platelets = Deliver (Hell-p, get the baby out!)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **B (Nifedipine, 37 weeks):** Antihypertensives manage blood pressure but do not treat HELLP. Waiting to 37 weeks with active HELLP is dangerous.
- **C (Expectant to 34 weeks):** Expectant management may be considered in mild preeclampsia at very early gestational ages, but NOT with active HELLP syndrome — the risk of maternal morbidity/mortality is too high.
- **D (Emergent CS without delay):** Vaginal delivery is actually preferred if possible. The mode of delivery depends on obstetric factors, but magnesium and stabilization should precede delivery planning. Emergent CS "without delay" bypasses necessary stabilization.
- **E (Observe 24 hrs):** HELLP syndrome can deteriorate rapidly. Observation without a delivery plan is inappropriate once HELLP is diagnosed.

### Question 3

A 55-year-old man with a 40 pack-year smoking history presents with progressive dyspnea on exertion and a chronic productive cough. Spirometry shows FEV1/FVC ratio of 0.62 with FEV1 48% of predicted. He currently uses a short-acting bronchodilator as needed. He had two exacerbations requiring oral steroids in the past year.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate modification to his management?**

A) Add inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) alone  
B) Add long-acting muscarinic antagonist + long-acting beta-agonist (LAMA + LABA)  
C) Add ICS + long-acting beta-agonist (LABA) combination  
D) Start oral theophylline  
E) Initiate roflumilast  

---

**Domain:** Respiratory System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Pharmacotherapy  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Add long-acting muscarinic antagonist + long-acting beta-agonist (LAMA + LABA)**

*Why it's correct:* Per GOLD 2024 guidance, this patient is a frequent exacerbator (Group E), and the preferred initial escalation is dual long-acting bronchodilation with **LAMA + LABA**. This reduces exacerbations and improves symptoms better than single-agent long-acting bronchodilator therapy. ICS-containing regimens are now primarily reserved for patients with blood eosinophils **>=300 cells/microL** (or overlapping asthma features), rather than routine first escalation in Group E.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "GOLD Group E = Expand bronchodilation first (LAMA + LABA), add ICS if eosinophils high"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (ICS alone):** ICS monotherapy is not appropriate for COPD — it is not recommended without a LABA due to increased pneumonia risk without commensurate benefit.
- **C (ICS + LABA):** No longer preferred as initial escalation for Group E in GOLD 2024 unless blood eosinophils are high (>=300 cells/microL) or there is concomitant asthma.
- **D (Theophylline):** Theophylline is a third-line agent with narrow therapeutic index and multiple drug interactions. It is not first-line escalation.
- **E (Roflumilast):** Roflumilast (a PDE4 inhibitor) can be added in chronic bronchitic phenotype with FEV1 <50% and frequent exacerbations, but it is an add-on therapy after ICS/LABA, not a replacement.

### Question 4

A 72-year-old woman is brought to the clinic by her daughter who says her mother has been "confused and forgetful" for the past 6 months. On exam, the patient scores 19/30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). She has stepwise cognitive decline with two previous episodes of sudden worsening followed by partial recovery. She has a history of hypertension and atrial fibrillation. Neurologic exam shows mild left-sided weakness and hyperreflexia.

**What is the most likely diagnosis?**

A) Alzheimer's dementia  
B) Lewy body dementia  
C) Frontotemporal dementia  
D) Vascular dementia  
E) Normal pressure hydrocephalus  

---

**Domain:** Nervous System & Special Senses  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Formulating the Diagnosis  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: D — Vascular dementia**

*Why it's correct:* The hallmark of vascular dementia is stepwise, abrupt deterioration corresponding to ischemic events, followed by partial recovery — unlike Alzheimer's, which is gradual and progressive. Risk factors (hypertension, atrial fibrillation → embolic strokes) and focal neurologic signs (left-sided weakness, hyperreflexia) strongly support a vascular etiology.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "STEPS = STEPwise = Strokes = Vascular dementia"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Alzheimer's):** Alzheimer's has insidious onset and gradual, progressive decline — not stepwise. Focal neurologic signs are absent early.
- **B (Lewy body):** Lewy body dementia features visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, Parkinsonism, and fluctuating cognition — not stepwise decline after strokes.
- **C (Frontotemporal):** FTD presents with personality/behavioral changes and language problems, typically in younger patients (50s–60s), without stroke-related stepwise pattern.
- **E (Normal pressure hydrocephalus):** NPH classic triad is "Wet, Wobbly, Wacky" (urinary incontinence, gait ataxia, dementia). Stepwise decline and stroke risk factors are not features.

### Question 5

A 28-year-old woman presents with a 2-day history of fever, dysuria, urinary frequency, and right flank pain. Temperature is 38.9°C. Urinalysis shows pyuria, bacteriuria, and WBC casts. Blood cultures are drawn. She is not pregnant.

**What is the most appropriate antibiotic therapy?**

A) Oral nitrofurantoin for 5 days  
B) Oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) for 3 days  
C) IV ceftriaxone followed by oral ciprofloxacin for 14 days total  
D) Oral amoxicillin-clavulanate for 7 days  
E) IV vancomycin for 10 days  

---

**Domain:** Renal & Urinary System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Pharmacotherapy  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — IV ceftriaxone followed by oral ciprofloxacin for 14 days total**

*Why it's correct:* This presentation is consistent with acute pyelonephritis (fever, flank pain, WBC casts = upper UTI). Patients with systemic signs (fever >38.5°C) and concern for bacteremia (blood cultures drawn) warrant initial IV antibiotics. Ceftriaxone is an appropriate initial IV agent. Once clinically improving and afebrile for 24–48 hours, transition to oral fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin) to complete 10–14 days total.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "PYELONE = Pyuria + flank pain + casts = hospital IV then oral Cipro"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Nitrofurantoin):** Nitrofurantoin does NOT achieve adequate tissue/renal concentrations — contraindicated in pyelonephritis. It is only for uncomplicated lower UTIs.
- **B (TMP-SMX 3 days):** Short-course therapy is for uncomplicated cystitis. This patient has upper tract disease and systemic illness — 3 days is grossly inadequate.
- **D (Amoxicillin-clavulanate):** Not first-line for pyelonephritis due to high resistance rates among E. coli; may be used if sensitivities confirm susceptibility.
- **E (IV vancomycin):** Vancomycin covers gram-positive organisms. The most common pyelonephritis pathogen is gram-negative E. coli — vancomycin provides no coverage.

### Question 6

A 45-year-old man with no past medical history presents with a 3-week history of fatigue, night sweats, and a 6 kg weight loss. On exam, he has painless cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy. A lymph node biopsy shows large cells with prominent "owl-eye" nuclei.

**Which of the following best describes the prognosis of this condition?**

A) Poor prognosis; 5-year survival is less than 20% with standard chemotherapy  
B) Excellent prognosis; most patients achieve cure with ABVD chemotherapy  
C) Variable prognosis; determined primarily by serum LDH level alone  
D) Favorable in early stage only; advanced disease is universally fatal within 2 years  
E) Prognosis is unaffected by stage at diagnosis  

---

**Domain:** Blood & Lymphoreticular System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Determining Prognosis/Outcome  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Excellent prognosis; most patients achieve cure with ABVD chemotherapy**

*Why it's correct:* "Owl-eye" Reed-Sternberg cells are pathognomonic for Hodgkin lymphoma. Hodgkin lymphoma has an excellent prognosis overall — it is one of the most curable cancers, with cure rates of 80–90% with ABVD (Adriamycin/doxorubicin, Bleomycin, Vinblastine, Dacarbazine) chemotherapy ± radiation. Even advanced-stage disease is often curable.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Hodgkin = HOPEful diagnosis (ABVD = A Beautiful Victory, Dude!)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (<20% survival):** This dramatically underestimates Hodgkin lymphoma's curability. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (especially aggressive subtypes) carries a worse prognosis.
- **C (LDH alone):** The International Prognostic Score (IPS) for advanced Hodgkin disease uses 7 factors (albumin, hemoglobin, sex, age, stage IV, WBC, lymphocyte count) — not LDH alone.
- **D (Advanced disease universally fatal):** False — even Stage IV Hodgkin lymphoma has 5-year survival rates of 65–75% with modern therapy.
- **E (Unaffected by stage):** Stage absolutely affects prognosis — Stage I/II has >90% cure rates vs lower rates in Stage III/IV, though still highly curable.

### Question 7

A 19-year-old college student presents with a 5-day history of sore throat, fever, and fatigue. Exam shows tonsillar exudates, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly. Monospot test is positive.

**What counseling is most important to give this patient?**

A) Start amoxicillin immediately to prevent complications  
B) Avoid contact sports for at least 3–4 weeks due to risk of splenic rupture  
C) Obtain LFTs weekly for 3 months to monitor for liver failure  
D) Administer corticosteroids to shorten the duration of illness  
E) Vaccinate close contacts immediately  

---

**Domain:** Blood & Lymphoreticular System  
**Competency:** Communication — Providing Information; Patient Care: Management  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Avoid contact sports for at least 3–4 weeks due to risk of splenic rupture**

*Why it's correct:* This is infectious mononucleosis (EBV). The most dangerous complication is splenic rupture, which can occur spontaneously but is precipitated by trauma or contact sports. Patients must be counseled to avoid contact sports and strenuous activity for at least 3–4 weeks (some guidelines say 4 weeks minimum with imaging confirmation of spleen normalization before return to play).

**🧠 Mnemonic: "MONO = Many Organs NOwhere to be touched (especially the SPLEEN)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Amoxicillin):** Amoxicillin in EBV mono causes a diffuse maculopapular rash in ~80% of cases — it is contraindicated unless a secondary bacterial infection is confirmed.
- **C (Weekly LFTs):** Mild transaminase elevation is common in mono; routine weekly LFTs are not indicated unless there is clinical concern for hepatitis.
- **D (Corticosteroids):** Steroids are reserved for specific complications (severe airway obstruction, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia) — not routine illness shortening.
- **E (Vaccinate contacts):** There is no EBV vaccine available. Quarantine or vaccination of contacts is not standard practice.

### Question 8

A 52-year-old man with a history of alcohol use disorder presents with hematemesis. He appears jaundiced. Vital signs: BP 95/60 mmHg, HR 120 bpm. Exam shows spider angiomata, palmar erythema, and prominent abdominal veins. After initial fluid resuscitation, upper endoscopy reveals actively bleeding esophageal varices.

**In addition to endoscopic band ligation, which pharmacologic agent should be started immediately?**

A) Omeprazole IV infusion  
B) Octreotide IV infusion  
C) Propranolol oral  
D) Vasopressin nasal spray  
E) Lactulose oral  

---

**Domain:** Gastrointestinal System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Pharmacotherapy (immediate management)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Octreotide IV infusion**

*Why it's correct:* In acute variceal hemorrhage, octreotide (somatostatin analogue) reduces portal hypertension by inhibiting vasodilatory hormones (glucagon), thereby decreasing splanchnic blood flow. It is given IV in the acute setting alongside endoscopic therapy and antibiotics (to prevent spontaneous bacterial peritonitis). It should be started even before endoscopy if variceal bleed is suspected.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "OCTreotide OCTopus squeezes portal vessels tight"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Omeprazole):** PPI infusion is appropriate for peptic ulcer bleed (non-variceal). It does not reduce portal pressure.
- **C (Propranolol oral):** Non-selective beta-blockers are used for PRIMARY and SECONDARY prophylaxis of variceal bleeding — not in the acute hemorrhage setting. They can cause hemodynamic instability.
- **D (Vasopressin nasal spray):** Vasopressin (or terlipressin) can be used IV for variceal bleeding in some settings, but nasal spray formulation is not appropriate for this acute emergency.
- **E (Lactulose):** Lactulose treats hepatic encephalopathy, not acute variceal hemorrhage.

### Question 9

A 44-year-old woman with lupus presents with a 2-week history of progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and leg swelling. Exam reveals elevated JVP, diffuse crackles, and pitting edema to the knees. Echo shows an EF of 25% with global hypokinesis. She has been taking hydroxychloroquine for 12 years.

**What is the most likely etiology of her cardiomyopathy?**

A) Hypertensive cardiomyopathy  
B) Alcoholic cardiomyopathy  
C) Hydroxychloroquine-induced cardiomyopathy  
D) Lupus myocarditis  
E) Ischemic cardiomyopathy from premature CAD  

---

**Domain:** Cardiovascular System / Immune System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Formulating the Diagnosis; Adverse Drug Effects  
**Difficulty:** Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Hydroxychloroquine-induced cardiomyopathy**

*Why it's correct:* Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) cardiotoxicity is classically a **chronic exposure/cumulative dose** complication. A 12-year exposure strongly raises concern for HCQ-induced cardiomyopathy (often with global dysfunction and/or conduction abnormalities). In contrast, lupus myocarditis is usually tied to an **acute inflammatory flare** pattern and does not require prolonged medication exposure. In this vignette, chronicity and cumulative HCQ burden are the major discriminators.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "HCQ = Hidden Cardiac Quirk (stores lipids in heart cells)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Hypertensive CMP):** No history of hypertension mentioned; hypertensive CMP typically presents with concentric hypertrophy, not global hypokinesis.
- **B (Alcoholic CMP):** No alcohol history; alcoholic CMP is a diagnosis of exclusion in a known heavy drinker.
- **D (Lupus myocarditis):** Lupus myocarditis remains in the differential, but the prolonged 12-year HCQ exposure and cumulative toxicity profile make drug-induced cardiomyopathy more likely here.
- **E (Ischemic CMP):** SLE does increase CAD risk, but global hypokinesis without focal wall motion abnormality is less characteristic of ischemic disease. HCQ is the most specific answer here.

### Question 10

A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and CKD (eGFR 28 mL/min/1.73m²) presents for routine follow-up. His HbA1c is 8.9%. His current regimen is metformin 1000 mg twice daily.

**What is the most appropriate change to his diabetes management?**

A) Increase metformin to 2000 mg twice daily  
B) Discontinue metformin; start insulin glargine  
C) Discontinue metformin; start a SGLT-2 inhibitor  
D) Add a GLP-1 receptor agonist to current metformin  
E) Discontinue metformin; add a sulfonylurea  

---

**Domain:** Endocrine System / Renal & Urinary System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Pharmacotherapy; Adverse Drug Effects  
**Difficulty:** Moderate-Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Discontinue metformin; start insulin glargine**

*Why it's correct:* Metformin is contraindicated (or should be discontinued) when eGFR falls below 30 mL/min/1.73m² due to risk of lactic acidosis. At eGFR 28, metformin must be stopped. Insulin glargine (basal insulin) is safe across all levels of kidney function and is appropriate for this patient with HbA1c significantly above target.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Metformin Must stop at eGFR <30 (Metformin = 30 limit)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Increase metformin):** Increasing the dose of a contraindicated medication is dangerous. Metformin is already contraindicated at this eGFR.
- **C (SGLT-2 inhibitor):** SGLT-2 inhibitors have reduced efficacy at eGFR <45 (empagliflozin/dapagliflozin) and are generally not recommended for glycemic control at eGFR <30 (though some have CKD-protective indications at lower eGFR with different thresholds — still, not first choice here).
- **D (GLP-1 agonist + metformin):** Metformin must be stopped first; cannot simply add to it.
- **E (Sulfonylurea):** Sulfonylureas (e.g., glipizide) increase risk of prolonged hypoglycemia in CKD due to reduced renal clearance — not ideal.

### Question 11

A 3-year-old boy is brought in by his parents for recurrent sinopulmonary infections since age 6 months. He has had pneumonia twice, requiring hospitalization. Labs show: IgG 180 mg/dL (normal >400), IgA undetectable, IgM undetectable. B cells are absent on flow cytometry. T cells are normal.

**What is the most likely diagnosis?**

A) Selective IgA deficiency  
B) Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)  
C) X-linked agammaglobulinemia (Bruton's disease)  
D) Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)  
E) Hyper-IgM syndrome  

---

**Domain:** Immune System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Formulating the Diagnosis  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — X-linked agammaglobulinemia (Bruton's disease)**

*Why it's correct:* X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA) is caused by a mutation in BTK (Bruton's tyrosine kinase), resulting in failure of B cell maturation. Key features: male patient, onset of infections after maternal IgG wanes (~6 months), absent B cells, very low/absent all immunoglobulin classes (IgG, IgA, IgM), normal T cells. This perfectly fits the presentation.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Bruton's Boys lack B cells — Born without Bs (BTK mutation)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Selective IgA deficiency):** Only IgA is low — IgG and IgM are normal/elevated. B cells are present. This patient has pan-hypogammaglobulinemia and no B cells.
- **B (CVID):** CVID presents similarly but typically manifests in the 2nd–3rd decade (not infancy), and B cells are usually present but dysfunctional.
- **D (SCID):** SCID has absent or dysfunctional T AND B cells; T cell count is normal in this patient.
- **E (Hyper-IgM syndrome):** Characterized by normal/elevated IgM but very low IgG and IgA. Here IgM is absent.

### Question 12

A 29-year-old woman presents with 6 months of amenorrhea, galactorrhea, and headaches. Serum prolactin is 180 ng/mL (normal <25). MRI of the pituitary shows a 1.2 cm mass with suprasellar extension. Visual field testing reveals bitemporal hemianopia.

**What is the most appropriate first-line treatment?**

A) Surgical resection via transsphenoidal approach  
B) Radiation therapy  
C) Cabergoline (dopamine agonist)  
D) Bromocriptine plus estrogen replacement  
E) Observation with serial MRIs  

---

**Domain:** Endocrine System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Cabergoline (dopamine agonist)**

*Why it's correct:* This is a macroprolactinoma (>1 cm). Even with visual field defects from suprasellar extension, the first-line treatment for prolactinomas (regardless of size) is dopamine agonist therapy — cabergoline (preferred due to better tolerability and efficacy) or bromocriptine. Dopamine agonists shrink tumors in most patients and can reverse visual field defects. Surgery is reserved for patients who fail or cannot tolerate medical therapy, or with CSF leak or pituitary apoplexy.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Prolactinoma = Pills before Procedure (Cabergoline FIRST, Surgery second)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Surgery first):** Surgery is NOT first-line for prolactinoma, even with visual field compromise — medical therapy typically resolves this.
- **B (Radiation):** Reserved for refractory cases or when surgery fails; not first-line.
- **D (Bromocriptine + estrogen):** Estrogen is not added — it would raise prolactin. Cabergoline alone is preferred over bromocriptine.
- **E (Observation):** A symptomatic macroprolactinoma with visual compromise requires active treatment.

### Question 13

A randomized controlled trial tests a new antihypertensive drug versus placebo. After 5 years, the rate of MI in the treatment group is 4% and in the placebo group is 8%. The study enrolls 500 patients per arm.

**What is the number needed to treat (NNT) to prevent one MI?**

A) 4  
B) 8  
C) 12  
D) 25  
E) 50  

---

**Domain:** Biostatistics, Epidemiology/Population Health  
**Competency:** Practice-based Learning — Biostatistics and Epidemiology  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: D — 25**

*Why it's correct:* NNT = 1 / Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR). ARR = 8% − 4% = 4% = 0.04. NNT = 1/0.04 = 25. You need to treat 25 patients for 5 years to prevent 1 MI.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "NNT = 1 over ARR (Never Neglect The ARR)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (4):** This is the event rate in the treatment group, not NNT.
- **B (8):** This is the event rate in the placebo group.
- **C (12):** Incorrect calculation.
- **E (50):** This would be 1/0.02 — confuses relative risk reduction with ARR.

### Question 14

A 58-year-old woman is brought in by her husband for 3 months of difficulty word-finding, writing errors, and getting lost in her own neighborhood. She was previously a high-functioning accountant. She has no history of stroke or substance use. MRI shows temporoparietal and posterior cingulate atrophy. PET scan shows bilateral temporoparietal hypometabolism.

**What is the most likely diagnosis?**

A) Vascular dementia  
B) Frontotemporal dementia  
C) Alzheimer's disease  
D) Lewy body dementia  
E) Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease  

---

**Domain:** Nervous System & Special Senses  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Formulating the Diagnosis  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Alzheimer's disease**

*Why it's correct:* Alzheimer's disease presents with insidious onset of episodic memory loss, language difficulties (aphasia), visuospatial deficits, and getting lost. The classic imaging findings are temporoparietal and posterior cingulate atrophy on MRI with corresponding hypometabolism on FDG-PET. The patient profile (late 50s, progressive cognitive decline, functional decline in a high-functioning individual) is typical.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "ALZheimer = A Loss of Z (temporoparietal atrophy) = FDG-PET hypometabolism in temporal-parietal lobes"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Vascular dementia):** No stroke history; stepwise pattern absent; no focal neurologic signs.
- **B (FTD):** Frontotemporal dementia primarily affects behavior/personality (frontal) or language (temporal variant), with frontal > temporal atrophy. Memory spared early.
- **D (Lewy body):** Lewy body dementia features Parkinsonism, visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder; imaging shows occipital hypometabolism.
- **E (CJD):** Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is rapidly progressive (weeks to months), with myoclonus, periodic sharp waves on EEG, and DWI cortical ribboning on MRI.

### Question 15

A 38-year-old male nurse presents requesting HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) after a needle stick injury 18 hours ago involving a patient known to be HIV-positive with a viral load of 50,000 copies/mL.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate management?**

A) Reassure him that needle stick transmission risk is very low and no PEP is needed  
B) Start two-drug PEP with zidovudine and lamivudine immediately  
C) Start three-drug PEP with tenofovir, emtricitabine, and raltegravir; continue for 28 days  
D) Test the healthcare worker for HIV first; start PEP only if negative  
E) Consult infectious disease and start PEP in 72 hours  

---

**Domain:** Immune System / Multisystem Processes  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Start three-drug PEP with tenofovir/emtricitabine/raltegravir for 28 days**

*Why it's correct:* PEP should be started as soon as possible (ideally within hours, definitely within 72 hours) after high-risk exposure. The recommended regimen per current CDC guidelines is preferred three-drug regimen: tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine (TDF/FTC) + raltegravir (or dolutegravir) for 28 days. The source patient's high viral load further supports PEP initiation.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "PEP = Prevent Early Plague — 3 drugs, 28 days, within 72 hours"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (No PEP needed):** Needle stick from HIV+ patient with high viral load is a significant exposure — dismissing PEP is inappropriate.
- **B (Two-drug PEP):** Two-drug regimens are no longer recommended; three-drug PEP is standard.
- **D (Test first):** Do not delay PEP to wait for HCW's test results — start PEP immediately. Testing the HCW establishes baseline but does not delay treatment.
- **E (72-hour delay):** 72 hours is the MAXIMUM window — not a target to delay. Start immediately.

### Question 16

A 67-year-old man with a history of COPD, hypertension, and BPH presents with a 2-day history of fever, productive cough with purulent sputum, and worsening dyspnea. CXR shows a right lower lobe consolidation. He is admitted to the general floor (non-ICU). He has no recent hospitalization.

**What is the most appropriate empiric antibiotic regimen for this hospitalized non-ICU CAP patient?**

A) Azithromycin monotherapy  
B) Amoxicillin-clavulanate monotherapy  
C) Either beta-lactam (e.g., ceftriaxone) plus a macrolide (e.g., azithromycin) OR respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy  
D) Ceftriaxone monotherapy

*Why it's correct:* SAAG = Serum albumin − Ascitic albumin = 3.8 − 0.9 = 2.9 g/dL. SAAG ≥1.1 indicates portal hypertension (cirrhosis, hepatic vein thrombosis, cardiac ascites, etc.). SAAG <1.1 suggests non-portal hypertensive causes (malignancy, TB, pancreatitis, nephrotic syndrome). WBC 150 with PMNs 40% = 60/µL PMNs (<250) — no spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "SAAG ≥1.1 = Sinusoidal (portal HT): 'High SAAG = High sinusoidal pressure'"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (<1.1, TB):** TB peritonitis has SAAG <1.1. Here SAAG is 2.9.
- **C (<1.1, malignancy):** Malignant ascites typically has SAAG <1.1. Here SAAG is 2.9.
- **D (>1.1, nephrotic):** Nephrotic syndrome causes SAAG <1.1 ascites (low-protein ascites without portal HTN).
- **E (<1.1, cardiac):** Cardiac ascites has SAAG >1.1 (correct category), but cardiac is not the best answer — also SAAG >1.1 is stated correctly in option B.

### Question 50

A 63-year-old man presents with 3 months of lower urinary tract symptoms: nocturia × 4, weak stream, hesitancy, and post-void dribbling. DRE shows a symmetrically enlarged, smooth, non-tender prostate. PSA is 3.2 ng/mL.

**What is the most appropriate initial pharmacologic management?**

A) Finasteride (5-alpha reductase inhibitor) alone  
B) Tamsulosin (alpha-1 blocker) alone  
C) Combination tamsulosin + finasteride  
D) Oxybutynin (anticholinergic)  
E) Doxazosin (alpha-1 blocker) alone  

---

**Domain:** Male Reproductive System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Pharmacotherapy  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: B — Tamsulosin (alpha-1 blocker) alone**

*Why it's correct:* This is benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). For initial symptom management, alpha-1 blockers (tamsulosin, silodosin) are first-line — they relax smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck within days. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride) reduce prostate volume but require 6+ months for effect and are best for large prostates (>40 mL) or elevated PSA. Combination therapy is for moderate-large BPH with elevated PSA or large volume.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Alpha Blockers = Acute relief (works fast); 5ARIs = Shrink slow (finasteride takes forever)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Finasteride alone):** Works for large glands; takes 6 months for symptom relief — not optimal for quick symptom management.
- **C (Combination):** Reserved for large prostate (>40 mL) or PSA >1.4 — data not provided suggesting this severity.
- **D (Oxybutynin):** Anticholinergics treat overactive bladder (urgency/frequency) but are relatively contraindicated in BPH as they can precipitate urinary retention.
- **E (Doxazosin):** Non-uroselective alpha-blocker with more BP-lowering side effects; tamsulosin is uroselective and better tolerated.
