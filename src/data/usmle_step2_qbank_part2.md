### Question 51

A 55-year-old man with a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia presents with sudden onset of the "worst headache of his life" that started 2 hours ago during exercise. He vomited once. Neurological exam is normal. Non-contrast head CT is negative.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A) Discharge home with analgesics and neurology follow-up  
B) MRI of the brain with gadolinium  
C) Lumbar puncture (LP)  
D) CT angiography of the head and neck  
E) Repeat CT in 6 hours  

---

**Domain:** Nervous System & Special Senses  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Laboratory and Diagnostic Studies  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Lumbar puncture**

*Why it's correct:* "Thunderclap headache" (sudden-onset worst-ever headache) must be evaluated for subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Non-contrast CT is ~98% sensitive within 6 hours but can miss small bleeds or those presenting later. A negative CT does NOT rule out SAH — LP is mandatory to look for xanthochromia (yellow CSF from RBC breakdown) or persistently elevated RBCs, which would confirm SAH.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Worst Headache Ever + Normal CT = LP to find Xanthochromia (WOH = LP!)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Discharge):** Premature and potentially fatal — SAH must be ruled out by LP after negative CT.
- **B (MRI with gadolinium):** MRI is less sensitive than CT for acute SAH in the first 24 hours; LP takes precedence.
- **D (CT angiography):** CTA can identify aneurysm location but only AFTER SAH is confirmed. Skip ahead to CTA after LP confirms SAH.
- **E (Repeat CT in 6 hours):** Not standard practice — LP should follow the initial negative CT.

### Question 52

A 48-year-old woman presents with progressive dysphagia to solids for 4 months. She has a long history of GERD treated with omeprazole. She has no weight loss. Upper endoscopy reveals a salmon-pink mucosal change in the distal esophagus extending 4 cm above the GEJ. Biopsy shows intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells.

**What is the most important next step in management?**

A) Increase omeprazole dose to twice daily and repeat endoscopy in 3 months  
B) Refer for surgical resection of the distal esophagus  
C) Surveillance endoscopy with biopsies per Barrett's protocol  
D) Start sucralfate and repeat endoscopy in 1 year  
E) Perform immediate radiofrequency ablation  

---

**Domain:** Gastrointestinal System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Health Maintenance (surveillance)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate-Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Surveillance endoscopy with biopsies per Barrett's protocol**

*Why it's correct:* This is Barrett's esophagus (intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells in the distal esophagus, a complication of chronic GERD). Management per ACG/AGA guidelines: optimize PPI therapy AND enroll in surveillance endoscopy protocol. For non-dysplastic Barrett's: endoscopy every 3–5 years. If low-grade dysplasia: every 6–12 months or ablation. High-grade dysplasia: ablation/resection.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Barrett's = GERD Goblet cells → Surveillance or Ablation depending on Dysplasia grade"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (More PPI, repeat in 3 months):** PPI optimization is appropriate, but the protocol requires structured surveillance, not just repeat EGD in 3 months.
- **B (Surgical resection):** Surgical resection is for high-grade dysplasia or early esophageal cancer — not non-dysplastic Barrett's.
- **D (Sucralfate + 1 year):** Inadequate for surveillance of a pre-malignant condition.
- **E (Immediate RFA):** Radiofrequency ablation is indicated for confirmed dysplastic Barrett's, not non-dysplastic disease.

### Question 53

A 6-week-old male infant is brought by his parents for projectile vomiting after every feeding for 2 weeks. He is hungry immediately after vomiting. On exam, the infant appears thin. A firm, olive-shaped mass is palpated in the epigastric area. Ultrasound shows a pyloric muscle thickness of 4 mm and channel length of 18 mm.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A) Immediately take patient to the OR for pyloromyotomy  
B) Change to soy-based formula  
C) Correct electrolyte abnormalities, then pyloromyotomy  
D) Start IV ranitidine and observe for 48 hours  
E) Trial of atropine therapy  

---

**Domain:** Gastrointestinal System / Human Development  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Correct electrolyte abnormalities, then pyloromyotomy**

*Why it's correct:* Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) presents classically: male infant (4:1 male:female), 2–8 weeks old, projectile non-bilious vomiting, hungry after vomiting, olive mass on exam. Vomiting causes hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. The surgical treatment is pyloromyotomy (Ramstedt procedure), but it is NOT an emergency — electrolyte abnormalities MUST be corrected first to prevent anesthetic complications.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Pyloric stenosis = Fix the Lytes FIRST, then Fix the Pylorus"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Immediate OR):** This is wrong — electrolyte abnormalities must be corrected first. Hypokalemia and alkalosis increase anesthetic/cardiac risk.
- **B (Soy formula):** This is for lactose intolerance or CMPA, not pyloric stenosis.
- **D (IV ranitidine):** Medical management does not treat HPS — surgery is definitive.
- **E (Atropine):** Some centers use atropine as medical management (relaxes pyloric muscle) but surgery remains gold standard.

### Question 54

### Question 55

A 29-year-old man presents with painless swelling of his left testicle for 3 months. Exam shows a hard, non-transilluminating left testicular mass. AFP is 2200 ng/mL, beta-hCG is 450 IU/L, LDH is elevated. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis shows retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy.

- **D (Testicular sparing):** Not appropriate for this size and clinical presentation.
- **E (BEP without surgery):** Orchiectomy is mandatory first for staging, histology, and local control.

### Question 57

A 66-year-old man with COPD presents with worsening dyspnea, increased sputum production, and purulent sputum for 3 days. Vitals: Temp 37.4°C, RR 22/min, SpO2 89% on room air. He appears in moderate distress. ABG: pH 7.31, PaCO2 62 mmHg, PaO2 54 mmHg.

**What is the most appropriate respiratory management?**

A) High-flow oxygen via non-rebreather mask at 15 L/min  
B) Intubation and mechanical ventilation  
C) Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (BiPAP)  
D) Helium-oxygen therapy (heliox)  
E) Supplemental oxygen at 1–2 L/min via nasal cannula  

---

**Domain:** Respiratory System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Management — Clinical Interventions  
**Difficulty:** Moderate-Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (BiPAP)**

*Why it's correct:* This patient has an acute COPD exacerbation with hypercapnic respiratory failure (pH 7.31, PaCO2 62). BiPAP (non-invasive ventilation) reduces the work of breathing, improves ventilation, reduces PaCO2, and has been shown to decrease intubation rates, mortality, and ICU stay in hypercapnic COPD exacerbations. It is the first-line intervention for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure in COPD.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "COPD + High CO2 + Low pH = BiPAP (Before Intubation, Apply Positive pressure)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (High-flow NRB):** High-flow oxygen without ventilatory support will not address hypercapnia and may actually worsen CO2 retention by blunting hypoxic drive in COPD.
- **B (Intubation):** Reserved for BiPAP failure, altered consciousness, or life-threatening deterioration — BiPAP should be tried first.
- **D (Heliox):** Heliox (helium-oxygen) may reduce work of breathing but is not first-line for this degree of hypercapnic failure.
- **E (1–2 L NC):** Appropriate oxygen delivery rate, but does not address the underlying hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis.

### Question 59

A 44-year-old woman with a history of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) presents with a 1-week history of worsening knee and ankle joint pain, new facial rash, and fatigue. She is taking hydroxychloroquine. Lab results: anti-dsDNA antibody titer is markedly elevated, C3 and C4 are decreased, and urinalysis shows 3+ proteinuria and RBC casts.

**What does the combination of low complement and RBC casts indicate?**

A) Lupus nephritis with active glomerulonephritis — nephrology referral and kidney biopsy needed  
B) Drug-induced lupus — discontinue hydroxychloroquine  
C) SLE flare without renal involvement  
D) Superimposed UTI with hematuria  
E) Antiphospholipid syndrome with renal thrombosis  

---

**Domain:** Renal & Urinary System / Immune System  
**Competency:** Patient Care: Diagnosis — Laboratory Studies; Prognosis/Outcome  
**Difficulty:** Hard  

**✅ Correct Answer: A — Lupus nephritis — nephrology referral and kidney biopsy needed**

*Why it's correct:* RBC casts in urinalysis are pathognomonic for glomerulonephritis. Combined with rising anti-dsDNA (disease activity marker), low complement (C3/C4 consumption by immune complexes), and heavy proteinuria — this is active lupus nephritis. - **D (UTI):** UTI does not cause RBC casts or low complement levels.

### Question 61

A physician is treating a patient with opioids for chronic cancer pain. During a follow-up visit, the patient says, "I want to be comfortable and I'm okay with higher doses even if it means I might die sooner." The physician is concerned about hastening death.

**What is the most appropriate ethical framework guiding the use of pain medication in this situation?**

A) Physician-assisted suicide — legally permitted in all states  
B) Euthanasia — the patient's consent makes it ethical  
C) Principle of double effect — high-dose opioids for comfort are ethical even if death may be hastened  
D) The physician should withhold opioids and refer to palliative care  
E) The patient lacks capacity; opioids are contraindicated  

---

**Domain:** Social Sciences / Behavioral Health  
**Competency:** Professionalism — Legal/Ethical Issues (death and dying)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  

**✅ Correct Answer: C — Principle of double effect**

*Why it's correct:* The Principle of Double Effect holds that an action with both a good effect (relieving pain) and a potential harmful effect (hastening death) is ethically permissible when: (1) the act itself is not inherently evil, (2) the intent is the good effect (comfort), (3) the good effect is not achieved via the bad effect, and (4) there is proportionate reason. Administering opioids for comfort care in terminal illness is a cornerstone of palliative medicine.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Double Effect = Dual outcome: Good Intent + Good Action = Ethically okay even with bad side effect"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Physician-assisted suicide):** PAS involves the physician providing means for the patient to end their own life — not the same as comfort opioids. Not legal in all states.
- **B (Euthanasia):** Euthanasia = physician directly administering a lethal dose to end life. Opioids for comfort ≠ euthanasia.
- **D (Withhold opioids):** Withholding comfort measures from a dying patient causes unnecessary suffering — unethical.
- **E (Lacks capacity):** No evidence of incapacity presented; patient is making an informed, autonomous request.

**🧠 Mnemonic: "Berkson = Hospital patients are Biased (sicker than general population)"**

**❌ Incorrect Answer Explanations:**

- **A (Recall bias):** Occurs when participants inaccurately remember past exposures — not relevant to measuring BP prevalence.
- **B (Lead-time bias):** Occurs in screening studies when earlier diagnosis falsely appears to improve survival — not applicable here.
- **D (Hawthorne effect):** Behavior changes because participants know they're being studied — not the primary concern here.
- **E (Observer bias):** Occurs when the researcher subjectively measures outcomes — less relevant when measuring BP objectively.
